Sunollo

Website Redesign

Sunollo is an early-stage solar company in Singapore, designing and installing residential systems built around each home.

I was the company’s only designer when the redesign began, working with the CTO on product and the CEO on direction. I took the site’s structure through three versions and designed the new product pages, Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care through to hi-fi, with a second designer taking the homepage from my wireframes.

Timeline
Jul – Dec 2024
What I did
UX/UI Design・Information Architecture・Marketing Design・Brand Research

02

The problem

Solar is unfamiliar and a big, long-term commitment — so nobody commits without understanding it first, and every discovery call had to build that understanding from scratch.

How might we give customers a way to cover the basics on their own time — so that no call has to start from zero?

Every prospect who inquires for Sunollo’s solar quotation was routed to the same place: a 1–2 hour video call with the CEO, cofounder, or CTO, walking through an 18-slide deck covering the Singapore energy market, Sunollo’s mission, its products, and financing — regardless of how much context that prospect actually needs.

Solar is a category most people have no mental model for, and it’s a big investment and a long-term commitment. That education genuinely had to happen.

But the call was doing two jobs that didn’t need to happen together:

  1. General education, which someone could reasonably absorb on their own time, and
  2. Home-specific solar quoting, which genuinely requires a real conversation.

Of the channels available:

  1. The live calls
  2. Custom solar proposal brochures
  3. The website
  4. Whatsapp

The website had the widest reach, publicly available to curious audiences anytime, anywhere. At the same time, it also carried the least educational content out of them all.

03

Brand into experience

Upon joining, Sunollo’s new brand identity had just been finished by a freelance brand designer. My role was to translate the brand guidelines and Sunollo’s core brand pillars into a user experience a customer can feel and see at any point of their journey with Sunollo.

My first project was the Custom Solar Proposal — the brochure Sunollo sent every customer who inquired for a solar quotation.

  • The CEO built each one manually in Pages, calculations in Excel — reformatting on the fly depending on how a call had gone
  • The CTO wanted it automated
  • I was asked to redesign the layout
  • How the automation would be built, and on what platform — I had no visibility into.

The CEO’s version used the wordmark, the tagline, the product naming, the color system. It was on-brand, but it still didn’t read premium. The problem wasn’t the brand — it was the structure underneath it. A brand guideline tells you what things should look like, but it doesn’t translate into cohesiveness.

I restructured the brochure to answer the following:

  1. Why solar and why Sunollo →
  2. How to switch →
  3. What are the particulars

Same brand guidelines, same content, different formatting.

The automation project was discontinued before anything was built, but what lasted was the research I did underneath it. I’d built a set of mind maps that stayed as my reference for every project.

  • How Sunollo functioned as a company
  • What each core pillar meant in practice
  • How the digital products could adapt

04

Three checkpoints

Throughout the project, the IA went through three working versions alongside requirements that kept moving.

V3.1

V2 put Sunollo’s entire product story on one page — one page doing six jobs: carrying USPs, product suite, financing table, installation steps, testimonials, FAQ, SunolloCare.

In V3.1, main and important topics have their own dedicated pages. The IA is structured in a way that the product carries the brand, About Us came out of the main nav to the footer.

V3.2

V3.2 is scoped into the product and lead-generation pages only. I traced user journeys to every possible lead generation page, taking that a customer could land on any of the pages first into consideration. This checkpoint covers the component-to-tier rethink and the segmentation email.

V3.3

The ideal state of Sunollo’s V3 IA I have drafted.

Almost every destination carries entry points and links to other related pages.

To see how the structure holds up, I worked in between the IA and mid-fidelity to test.

What kept moving

Three business changes that landed inside the design sprint:

The segmentation email

Days after I presented V3.1, three new product tiers — Radiance, Abundance, Abundance Pro — defined by energy usage and system cost.

Small, Medium, and Large

Added by the CEO later in the same phase. Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro each come in S, M, L configurations

Green Buildings became Sunollo Pro

A rebrand initiated by the cofounder between V3.2 and V3.3. Its offering wasn’t completely defined internally, so I kept the footprint light, only a single high-level page. The rebrand changed its name and nothing else.

The work ran page by page. Design a page, QA the build with the engineer, move to the next.

Things I deliberately skipped

GreenStore — parked at V3.1 pending direction on its long-term future. Never came back before launch.

Our Projects — For marketing purposes, I proposed an updated process for gathering information from each signed customer, since V2’s version was only installation photos. Unfortunately, the team cannot absorb the extra work, so I left it out of the redesign and reused V2’s project pages.

Hybrid payments — business required payment schemes kept simple for the first launch, so the plans comparison shows subscription and upfront only.

Draw your roof — Sunollo’s DIY roof-mapping tool. Until the scope was settled and the platform was live, there was nothing to link to.

05

The reference that kept breaking

(1)

My first draft of V3.1 IA organized Sunollo’s hardware by component: separate entry points for solar panels, optimizers, inverters, EV chargers, etc — leading each effectively their own landing pages.

Goal:

  • Showcase product superiority: build Sunollo’s brand reputation with its high-performing products as its front runners.

Supporting design reasoning:

  • Customers buy based on the trustworthiness and reputation of brands.
  • Take Apple and Anker’s landing pages as reference, product-forward and product-centric.

Sunollo’s hardware anchored to Performant, Beautiful, and Reliable are its brand pillars expressed as structure.

Buying a Macbook experience: you don’t assemble one part by part — you choose from pre-configured bundles, each built for a type of user.

When I presented the V3.1 draft, the CEO reached for a different comparison: buying a Macbook. That was the model he wanted reflected on the site, more as a principle than a literal instruction. I realized the IA needed rework.

Modeling Sunollo’s site on Apple’s pick-a-product experience risked implying customers were meant to choose their own components, when in reality Sunollo designs a system for a customer’s home, not the other way around.

V3.1 format assumes a customer knows what each product is and is choosing within it.

About a week into the IA rework, a new requirement landed on top of it:

CEO introducing new product tiers — Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro. These are tiers of pre-packaged solar solutions that cater to residential homes.

The IA had to accommodate both the structural restructuring and a brand-new product tier system.

I proposed bucketing the three under one navigation label: Home Solutions. On the other hand, Sunollo’s commercial line already had a category of its own (Sunollo Pro).

But the goal earlier — putting Sunollo’s products at the forefront of the brand, still needed a home. Rather than standalone landing pages by function, I moved it inside the tier pages themselves. Every Home Solutions page carries a Specifications section: a carousel of Sunollo’s hardware and specs.

The reference was applied wrong in navigation: Navigation asks you to choose.

The reference was applied correctly in content: Content shows as proof.

The components survived as navigation too, but one level down. Each Home Solutions page carries a secondary nav — only serves as wayfinding through a long page, and no longer implies assembling one;

(2)

By V3.3 I’d reversed the product-forward premise on the homepage and dedicated a section for About Sunollo. Apple and Anker are widely recognized brands, so they can lead and persuade with their products. Solar has no such baseline — before products can persuade anyone, the page has to establish first who Sunollo is and why solar at all.

With the three tiers accessible via the main navigation, a new question followed: should a customer’s path through the site influence the form they need to fill in when inquiring?

06

Comprehension at the point of choosing

That question had a structural cause: three Home Solutions tier pages, Plans & Pricing, and Sunollo Care were are all accessible from the main navigation, so a customer could possibly start their eventual inquiry from any of these pages.

I mapped the three possible entry-point flows, tracing how a customer might end up sending an inquiry from any of these pages.

  • Some would arrive with a tier already in mind;
  • Others would come from Plans & Pricing, or Sunollo Care; or
  • With no tier at all.

Component-wise, through the inquiry form — these came down to how much an inquiry should ask of a customer — a specific inquiry, or room to simply reach out.

2 problems surfaced:

  1. From the customer’s perspective: solar is a low-familiarity purchase for most people, and asking someone to self-select between Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro before they send an inquiry risks losing them to confusion.
  2. From the business side: even a pre-packaged system still runs through a home-specific design process, so there was a possibility that the pre-packaged wouldn’t survive contact with an actual roof — which raised the question of how much the tier distinction was even worth surfacing, if it may change anyway.

I brought this up to the CTO, along with the entry point flows. He made the call quickly — use one generic inquiry form across the entire site. We weren’t going to overcomplicate the first launch.

A new business requirement came: each tier would also come in Small, Medium, and Large configurations.

The lever available to me as a designer was the decision moment itself: simplify the information a customer encounters when they’re choosing between system sizes and financing plans.

How do customers interpret the different system sizes and financing options — and does that information give them enough clarity to inquire, or enough confusion to hold back?

The Home Solutions hub got a comparison table across all three tiers, so the differences were visible side by side before anyone went a level deeper.

Each tier page presented the configurations by asking a consultative question instead of plainly listing options — Which Abundance Pro is the best for your home? — with roof space and estimated monthly savings as the figures to decide on. And directly beneath the three cards, at the exact point where someone unsure would stall: Need something tailored? Let Sunollo customize the perfect fit for your home.

The tier asked customers to classify themselves, so I had to design as simply and explicitly as possible. The comparison made the difference visible; the tailored option meant choosing was not mandatory.

07

Teaching by depiction

From the first IA draft, the unfamiliarity problem was one of the main pressing issues to relieve for the website redesign.

Solar unfamiliarity is one of the problems the redesign most needed to solve. By the time I was designing the hi-fi product pages, explaining a power optimizer to someone who doesn’t know what one is takes a paragraph they probably won’t read.

So the pages kept equal focus on explaining and showing, both at the same time.

The optimizer section is two photographs of the same roof, side by side. On the left, labelled Eclipse Panels + SunMax Optimizers, one panel is marked with a red X and every other panel carries a green check. On the right, labelled Others, one panel is X’d and the whole array is X’d with it. The captions are one line each: each panel performs at its peak regardless of what’s happening with the others, against when one panel is shaded, whole system’s energy production is dragged down.

That’s what an optimizer does. No definition, no jargon, nothing to look up.

The same move runs through the page. The panels section photographs a tree shadow falling across an array and captions it More energy, even under shade — partial shading optimization, shown rather than named. The battery section traces glowing energy-flow lines from the roof through the inverter to the battery and the car, with a toggle between day and night. The inverter isn’t explained as DC-to-AC conversion; it’s called the brain. Smart home ready lists a freezer, a dishwasher, a pool pump, a television — not “connected appliances.”

Each of those sections was a short story about one component, told before any specifications appeared, and each did two jobs:

  1. showed what the component does, and
  2. made the case that Sunollo’s was better.

That’s where the pillars landed. Performant and Beautiful showed up together in lines like “Designed to impress, Engineered to excel,” backed by a number — “15% more sunshine captured,” against typical panels. Safe was stated outright: “For your home, safety always comes first.” Reliable was framed as always-on: “Keeping your home powered, day until night.”

At the top of each page, one image carries all of it: a photograph of a home with every component marked in place, hotspot by hotspot. Roof panels, inverter on the wall, charger in the carport, devices indoors. A list of component names presumes you know the words. A photograph doesn’t.

Underneath all of it sits the layer for people who do want the technical detail. Every page carries the

Specifications carousel — real hardware, real numbers, the specs rather than the pitch. Optional add-ons are footnoted at the section they appear in, so someone deep in a long page always knows what’s standard.

Not everyone reads spec sheets. That was fine — the goal wasn’t universal comprehension, it was universal access. Whether someone wanted the story or the numbers, both now existed on the same page, somewhere neither had before.

Leveraging aesthetics

Most of Sunollo’s customers own landed homes, skew upscale, and care a great deal how their house looks — the CEO was clear that industrial-looking solar was a reason people said no. So the hero is a dusk shot of a modern home with black-on-black panels that nearly disappear into the roof. Nobody writes “our panels look good.” The photograph settles it.

I couldn’t give customers the vocabulary. What I could do was build pages that didn’t need them to have it.

I designed each page to carry its own explanation — no prior knowledge of solar terms required.

08

Making the case for Sunollo Care

Sunollo Care was the after-sales service behind every system. It protected a customer’s savings, covered the costs a product warranty leaves out, and kept the system checked, monitored and current for the length of the contract. Once the panels were up, keeping them working was Sunollo’s job rather than the homeowner’s.

In V2, Sunollo Care’s benefits sat diluted in a small section on a page, and in the proposal brochure it occupies a header and a photo. Problem is, customers read it as an optional extra, which, positionally, is what it looked like.

But the business and the customers wanted different things:

  1. Sunollo preferred subscription plans —
    1. It kept the company hands-on with supporting every system after installation,
    2. Recurring income on top of the sale
  2. Customers tended to prefer buying outright

UX anchor:

How do you get someone to want something the business needs them to want, without it becoming a hard sell?

I didn’t try to push anyone off the plan they preferred. Upfront was a perfectly good sale. But it was the only plan where Care was optional, which made it the one decision where both sides could still meet. So that’s where the design put its weight.

The plans comparison never shipped, so I never learned how many upfront buyers would have added Care.

09

What Shipped

The ideal IA for the redesign had five main destinations. What launched had three.

The homepage, the Home Solutions hub, and all three tier pages — Radiance, Abundance, Abundance Pro went live in late October – early November.

These pages had to launch first as it did not exist in any other channel — not the Custom Solar Brochures, not the deck, and not in website V2. Then I moved to designing Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care. Both reached complete hi-fi, but neither entered a development sprint: our only engineer dropped to one a day per week and left mid-December.

What launched lacked crucial parts of what I have designed.

Set against how I’d designed the homepage — Why → How → What, the funnel broke:

The product pages went live while plans & financing and SunolloCare were still in design progress. Discussions with potential customers usually involved those topics, which made the case for getting those pages out.

So what launched did little to serve the problem redesign existed for. Even analytics from the shipped pages would’ve lacked substantial insights — that’d only have measured the product (what) layer. A huge part meant to shorten the calls never got shipped to test.
