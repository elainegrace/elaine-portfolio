Sunollo

Website Redesign

Sunollo is an early-stage solar company in Singapore, designing and installing residential systems built around each home.

I was the company’s only designer when the redesign began, working with the CTO on product and the CEO on direction. I took the site’s structure through three versions and designed the new product pages, Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care through to hi-fi, with a second designer taking the homepage from my wireframes.

Timeline
Jul – Dec 2024
What I did
UX/UI Design・Information Architecture・Marketing Design・Brand Research

02

The problem

Solar is unfamiliar and a big, long-term commitment — so nobody commits without understanding it first, and every discovery call had to build that understanding from scratch.

How might we give customers a way to cover the basics on their own time — so that no call has to start from zero?

Every prospect who inquires for Sunollo’s solar quotation was routed to the same place: a 1–2 hour video call with the CEO, cofounder, or CTO, walking through an 18-slide deck covering the Singapore energy market, Sunollo’s mission, its products, and financing — regardless of how much context that prospect actually needs.

Solar is a category most people have no mental model for, and it’s a big investment and a long-term commitment. That education genuinely had to happen.

But the call was doing two jobs that didn’t need to happen together:

  1. General education, which someone could reasonably absorb on their own time, and
  2. Home-specific solar quoting, which genuinely requires a real conversation.

Of the channels available:

  1. The live calls
  2. Custom solar proposal brochures
  3. The website
  4. Whatsapp

The website had the widest reach, publicly available to curious audiences anytime, anywhere. At the same time, it also carried the least educational content out of them all.

03

Brand into experience

Upon joining, Sunollo’s new brand identity had just been finished by a freelance brand designer. My role was to translate the brand guidelines and Sunollo’s core brand pillars into a user experience a customer can feel and see at any point of their journey with Sunollo.

My first project was the Custom Solar Proposal — the brochure Sunollo sent every customer who inquired for a solar quotation.

  • The CEO built each one manually in Pages, calculations in Excel — reformatting on the fly depending on how a call had gone
  • The CTO wanted it automated
  • I was asked to redesign the layout
  • How the automation would be built, and on what platform — I had no visibility into.

The CEO’s version used the wordmark, the tagline, the product naming, the color system. It was on-brand, but it still didn’t read premium. The problem wasn’t the brand — it was the structure underneath it. A brand guideline tells you what things should look like, but it doesn’t translate into cohesiveness.

I restructured the brochure to answer the following:

  1. Why solar and why Sunollo →
  2. How to switch →
  3. What are the particulars

Same brand guidelines, same content, different formatting.

The automation project was discontinued before anything was built, but what lasted was the research I did underneath it. I’d built a set of mind maps that stayed as my reference for every project.

  • How Sunollo functioned as a company
  • What each core pillar meant in practice
  • How the digital products could adapt

04

Three checkpoints

Throughout the project, the IA went through three working versions alongside requirements that kept moving.

V3.1

V2 put Sunollo’s entire product story on one page — one page doing six jobs: carrying USPs, product suite, financing table, installation steps, testimonials, FAQ, SunolloCare.

In V3.1, main and important topics have their own dedicated pages. The IA is structured in a way that the product carries the brand, About Us came out of the main nav to the footer.

V3.2

V3.2 is scoped into the product and lead-generation pages only. I traced user journeys to every possible lead generation page, taking that a customer could land on any of the pages first into consideration. This checkpoint covers the component-to-tier rethink and the segmentation email.

V3.3

The ideal state of Sunollo’s V3 IA I have drafted.

Almost every destination carries entry points and links to other related pages.

To see how the structure holds up, I worked in between the IA and mid-fidelity to test.

What kept moving

Three business changes that landed inside the design sprint:

The segmentation email

Days after I presented V3.1, three new product tiers — Radiance, Abundance, Abundance Pro — defined by energy usage and system cost.

Small, Medium, and Large

Added by the CEO later in the same phase. Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro each come in S, M, L configurations

Green Buildings became Sunollo Pro

A rebrand initiated by the cofounder between V3.2 and V3.3. Its offering wasn’t completely defined internally, so I kept the footprint light, only a single high-level page. The rebrand changed its name and nothing else.

The work ran page by page. Design a page, QA the build with the engineer, move to the next.

Things I deliberately skipped

GreenStore — parked at V3.1 pending direction on its long-term future. Never came back before launch.

Our Projects — For marketing purposes, I proposed an updated process for gathering information from each signed customer, since V2’s version was only installation photos. Unfortunately, the team cannot absorb the extra work, so I left it out of the redesign and reused V2’s project pages.

Hybrid payments — business required payment schemes kept simple for the first launch, so the plans comparison shows subscription and upfront only.

Draw your roof — Sunollo’s DIY roof-mapping tool. Until the scope was settled and the platform was live, there was nothing to link to.

05

The reference that kept breaking

(1)

My first draft of V3.1 IA organized Sunollo’s hardware by component: separate entry points for solar panels, optimizers, inverters, EV chargers, etc — leading each effectively their own landing pages.

Goal:

  • Showcase product superiority: build Sunollo’s brand reputation with its high-performing products as its front runners.

Supporting design reasoning:

  • Customers buy based on the trustworthiness and reputation of brands.
  • Take Apple and Anker’s landing pages as reference, product-forward and product-centric.

Sunollo’s hardware anchored to Performant, Beautiful, and Reliable are its brand pillars expressed as structure.

Buying a Macbook experience: you don’t assemble one part by part — you choose from pre-configured bundles, each built for a type of user.

When I presented the V3.1 draft, the CEO reached for a different comparison: buying a Macbook. That was the model he wanted reflected on the site, more as a principle than a literal instruction. I realized the IA needed rework.

Modeling Sunollo’s site on Apple’s pick-a-product experience risked implying customers were meant to choose their own components, when in reality Sunollo designs a system for a customer’s home, not the other way around.

V3.1 format assumes a customer knows what each product is and is choosing within it.

About a week into the IA rework, a new requirement landed on top of it:

CEO introducing new product tiers — Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro. These are tiers of pre-packaged solar solutions that cater to residential homes.

The IA had to accommodate both the structural restructuring and a brand-new product tier system.

I proposed bucketing the three under one navigation label: Home Solutions. On the other hand, Sunollo’s commercial line already had a category of its own (Sunollo Pro).

But the goal earlier — putting Sunollo’s products at the forefront of the brand, still needed a home. Rather than standalone landing pages by function, I moved it inside the tier pages themselves. Every Home Solutions page carries a Specifications section: a carousel of Sunollo’s hardware and specs.

The reference was applied wrong in navigation: Navigation asks you to choose.

The reference was applied correctly in content: Content shows as proof.

The components survived as navigation too, but one level down. Each Home Solutions page carries a secondary nav — only serves as wayfinding through a long page, and no longer implies assembling one;

(2)

By V3.3 I’d reversed the product-forward premise on the homepage and dedicated a section for About Sunollo. Apple and Anker are widely recognized brands, so they can lead and persuade with their products. Solar has no such baseline — before products can persuade anyone, the page has to establish first who Sunollo is and why solar at all.

With the three tiers accessible via the main navigation, a new question followed: should a customer’s path through the site influence the form they need to fill in when inquiring?
