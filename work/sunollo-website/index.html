Sunollo

Website Redesign

Sunollo is an early-stage solar company in Singapore, designing and installing residential systems built around each home.

I was the company’s only designer when the redesign began, working with the CTO on product and the CEO on direction. I took the site’s structure through three versions and designed the new product pages, Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care through to hi-fi, with a second designer taking the homepage from my wireframes.

My role
UI/UX Designer
Timeline
Jul – Dec 2024
What I did
Information Architecture・User Flows・UI Design・Content Design・Brand Research・Design QA

02

The problem

Solar. An unfamiliar category, a big investment, a long-term commitment. Before anyone commits, customers need to understand what they’re buying. Sunollo fits that entire picture into a single call.

How might we give customers a way to cover the basics on their own time — so that no call has to start from zero?

The call was doing two jobs that didn’t need to happen together:

  1. General education, which someone could reasonably absorb on their own time, and
  2. Home-specific solar quoting, which genuinely requires a real conversation.

Of the channels available:

The website had the widest reach, publicly available to prospects anytime, anywhere. At the same time, it also carried the least educational content out of them all.

03

Brand into experience

I joined just after Sunollo’s rebrand. My role was to bring the brand pillars into user experience, in every part of the customer journey, starting with the Custom Solar Proposal, the brochure sent to everyone who asked for a quote.

I restructured the brochure to answer the following:

  1. Why solar and why Sunollo
  2. How to switch
  3. What are the particulars

Same brand guidelines, same content, different formatting. The mind maps I built for structuring the brochure stayed as my reference for the website redesign.

04

Three IA checkpoints

As the requirements changed during the sprint, the IA changed with it, across three working versions: V3.1, V3.2 and V3.3.

V3.1

V2 put Sunollo’s entire product story on one page: USPs, product suite, financing table, installation steps, testimonials, FAQ and SunolloCare. V3.1 gave each major topic its own page. Product led the navigation, and About Us moved from the main nav to the footer.

V3.2

Between V3.1 and V3.2, the CEO introduced three product tiers: Radiance, Abundance and Abundance Pro, each a pre-packaged system for residential homes. V3.1 had grouped Sunollo’s hardware by function, so the IA needed restructuring around the tiers.

V3.2 focused on the product and lead-generation pages. Every page was treated as a possible landing page, with a traced route to lead generation from each. Mapping those routes in full was a way of checking how the V2 inquiry form held up under the new structure, and whether there was a case for revisiting it.

V3.3

V3.3 was the ideal-state IA, in which every page linked to its related pages. I tested the structure by moving back and forth between the IA and mid-fidelity wireframes.

What kept moving

Three business changes that landed inside the design sprint:

New product tiers — Days after I presented V3.1, three new product tiers — Radiance, Abundance, Abundance Pro — defined by energy usage and system cost.

Small, Medium, and Large — Added by the CEO later in the same phase. Radiance, Abundance, and Abundance Pro each come in S, M, L configurations

Green Buildings became Sunollo Pro — A rebrand initiated by the cofounder between V3.2 and V3.3. Its offering wasn’t completely defined internally, so I kept the footprint light, only a single high-level page. The rebrand changed its name and nothing else.

Things I deliberately skipped

GreenStore — parked at V3.1 pending direction on its long-term future. Never came back before launch.

Our Projects — For marketing purposes, I proposed an updated process for gathering information from each signed customer, since V2’s version was only installation photos. Unfortunately, the team cannot absorb the extra work, so I left it out of the redesign and reused V2’s project pages.

Hybrid payments — business required payment schemes kept simple for the first launch, so the plans comparison shows subscription and upfront only.

Draw your roof — Sunollo’s DIY roof-mapping tool. Until the scope was settled and the platform was live, there was nothing to link to.

05

The reference that kept breaking

In the navigation

My first draft of the V3.1 IA organized Sunollo’s hardware by component, with solar panels, optimizers, inverters and EV chargers each getting their own landing page.

The goal was to lead with product superiority and build Sunollo’s reputation on the performance of its hardware.

Customers are known to buy from brands they trust, and the product itself can earn that trust.

Apple and Anker were the reference points, both building their sites around the product and letting it carry the brand.

When I presented the draft, the CEO reached for a different comparison: buying a MacBook. You don’t assemble one part by part; you choose from a few pre-configured bundles, each built for a type of user. He meant it as a principle rather than an instruction, but it was enough to show the IA needed rework.

Modeling Sunollo’s site on Apple’s pick-a-product experience risked implying customers were meant to choose their own components, when in reality Sunollo designs a system for a customer’s home, not the other way around.

The IA now had to absorb a structural rework and a new tier system at the same time.

Since V2, residential solar had only ever been one product, so it never needed a category. In V3.2 with the tiers, it now had to sit apart from Green Buildings, the commercial and industrial line. I proposed Home Solutions and placed it first, as Sunollo’s core market.

Navigation asks you to choose. Content shows proof.

On the homepage

Solar has no such baseline — before products can persuade anyone, the page has to establish first who Sunollo is and why solar at all.

By V3.3, I’d reversed the product-forward premise on the homepage and gave About Sunollo a section of its own.

Apple and Anker are widely recognized brands, so they can lead and persuade with their products.

With the three tiers accessible via the main navigation, a new question followed: should a customer’s path through the site influence the form they need to fill in when inquiring?

06

Comprehension at the point of choosing

That question had a structural cause: the three Home Solutions tier pages, Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care were all reachable from the main navigation, so a customer could start their eventual inquiry from any of them.

I mapped three possible entry-point flows to trace where those inquiries would begin:

  • Some would arrive from a tier page, with a tier already in mind.
  • Others would arrive from Plans & Pricing or Sunollo Care, with no tier at all.

For the inquiry form, that came down to how much it should ask of a customer: a specific inquiry, or room to simply reach out. Two problems surfaced:

  1. For the customer: solar is a low-familiarity purchase for most people, and asking someone to choose between Radiance, Abundance and Abundance Pro risks losing them to confusion.
  2. For the business: even a pre-packaged system still goes through a home-specific design process, so the package a customer picked might not survive contact with an actual roof — which raised the question of how much the tier distinction was worth surfacing, if it might change anyway.

I brought both to the CTO along with the flows. He made the call quickly: one generic inquiry form across the entire site. We weren’t going to overcomplicate the first launch.

Then a new requirement arrived: each tier would also come in Small, Medium and Large configurations — nine combinations in all.

The form was settled. What I could still shape was the decision moment itself — the information a customer meets while choosing between system sizes and financing plans. The question I was designing against:

How do customers interpret the different system sizes and financing options — and does that information give them enough clarity to inquire, or enough confusion to hold back?

07

Teaching by depiction

Solar unfamiliarity is one of the problems the redesign most needed to solve. By the time I was designing the hi-fi product pages, explaining a power optimizer to someone who doesn’t know what one is takes a paragraph they probably won’t read.

So the pages kept equal focus on explaining and showing, both at the same time.

That’s what an optimizer does. No definition, no jargon, nothing to look up.

Brand pillars

Each of those sections also carried one of Sunollo’s four brand pillars, written into the copy rather than stated as values.

Full ecosystem

At the top of each page, one image brings it all together.

For viewers to recognize every part of the ecosystem, before they get introduced to what it’s called.

Specifications

Underneath all of it sits a layer for people who do want the technical detail.

The goal wasn’t universal comprehension. It was universal access.

Not everyone reads spec sheets, and that was fine. Whether someone wanted the story or the numbers, both now existed on the same page, somewhere neither had before.

Leveraging aesthetics

Most of Sunollo’s customers own landed homes, tend to be upscale, and care a great deal how their house looks — the CEO was clear that industrial-looking solar was a reason people said no.

08

Making the case for Sunollo Care

How do you get someone to want something the business needs them to want, without it becoming a hard sell?

Sunollo Care was the after-sales service behind every system. It guaranteed a customer’s savings, covered the costs a product warranty leaves out, and kept the system checked, monitored and current for the length of the contract. Once the panels were up, keeping them clean and running at their best became Sunollo’s job.

As the CTO had flagged, customers assumed a product warranty already covered everything.

  • On V2, Care’s benefits sat in a small section on a page about something else;
  • In the CEO’s proposal, it got a header and a photo.

Customers read it as an optional extra, which, positionally, is what it looked like.

The business and its customers wanted different things:

  • Sunollo preferred subscription plans, which included Care — keeping the company involved with every system after installation, and bringing recurring income on top of the sale.
  • Customers tended to prefer buying outright, so the system was theirs, with nothing still owed.

Upfront was a perfectly good sale, so I didn’t try to push anyone off the plan they preferred. But it was the only plan where Care was optional, which made it the one decision where both sides could still meet. So that’s where the design put its weight.

The plans comparison never shipped, so I never learned how many upfront buyers would have added Care.

09

What Shipped

The ideal IA for the redesign had five main destinations. What launched had three.

The homepage, the Home Solutions hub, and all three tier pages — Radiance, Abundance, Abundance Pro went live in late October – early November.

These pages had to launch first as it did not exist in any other channel — not the Custom Solar Brochures, not the deck, and not in website V2. Then I moved to designing Plans & Pricing and Sunollo Care. Both reached complete hi-fi, but neither entered a development sprint: our only engineer dropped to one a day per week and left mid-December.

What launched lacked crucial parts of what I have designed.

Set against how I’d designed the homepage — Why → How → What, the funnel broke:

The product pages went live while plans & financing and SunolloCare were still in design progress. Discussions with potential customers usually involved those topics, which made the case for getting those pages out.

So what launched did little to serve the problem redesign existed for. Even analytics from the shipped pages would’ve lacked substantial insights — that’d only have measured the product (what) layer. A huge part meant to shorten the calls never got shipped to test.

10

What I’d measure

Each Home Solutions page offers three configurations — Small, Medium and Large — with the tailored option sitting underneath them. That set up the question I never got to settle:

Do the tiers and their configurations help a customer understand what they’re buying, or do they add more to understand?

My plan was to design for the most comprehensible state I could, launch, watch how customers actually used the pages, and let that evidence decide whether the website needed to change course.

To back that with evidence, I needed two things:

  1. Volume. Website inquiries were rare in the early weeks after launch — too few to read a pattern from. The other routes bringing traffic to the site would have been worth looking at as well.
  2. Time. I was reassigned to kick off research for another Sunollo project before a pattern could form.

Had I had both, these are the questions I’d have looked into:

  • How often was the tailored option chosen over the three configurations? That’s a direct read on whether people could place themselves among the tiers.
  • Did customers sign for the tier they’d inquired about? If they did, the tiers were segmenting real needs; if they didn’t, either the front-facing design or the offering itself needed rethinking.
  • What did people still ask on WhatsApp after inquiring about a specific tier?
  • How did the call duration change, before and after?
  • With nine defined configurations, did a real roof still need a retrofit — undercutting the intent behind the tiers?

Resolving these fully would have meant stepping into product strategy, which product design doesn’t own alone.

11

Close

By the time I was reassigned, the education layer still hadn’t been built. The other designer moved to the same project, so there was no one left on the website at all — and as far as I know it was never picked up again.

What shipped covered less than the IA I designed. The IA explained why solar made sense and how to pay for it, alongside the products themselves; what shipped was the products and a new homepage. The thinking behind the rest still holds — the website was never meant to replace the discovery call, only to let the channel that can scale carry more of the weight, so the call starts further along than zero.

Half of that shipped. The half that didn’t is still the half that mattered most.
